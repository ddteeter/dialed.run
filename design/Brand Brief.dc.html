00 / The frame

Runners solve the same dressing problem 300 times a year, from memory. We turn that memory into data.

CATEGORY TRUTH

Every runner owns 40+ pieces of technical clothing and mentally re-derives the same rules every morning.

THE GAP

Training apps log the run. Nobody logs the kit. The most-repeated decision in the sport is the least-instrumented.

OUR CLAIM

Your closet, indexed by weather and by what actually worked — yours and everyone else's.

01 / Name rationale
Decided
[dialed.run]

Names the verdict, not the garment. It's already the word runners use for a nailed outfit, so the name, the success state, and the payoff of the recommendation collapse into one idea. Spells itself on first hearing, and it's native slang in cycling and skiing too — so the word survives a second sport where singlet could not.

SAY IT"Get dialed." Lowercase always, including at the start of a sentence.
HOLDdialed.bike, dialed.ski, dialed.cc — cheap insurance, no commitment.
WATCH FORThe name says nothing about clothing alone. The tagline has to carry that.

How we got here — the five candidates considered, kept for the record. All were available on .run, a TLD that does real work here, reading as a verb rather than a domain hack.

singlet.run

Reads as: an insider noun. A singlet is the most iconic piece of running clothing there is — earned, race-day, tribal.

Strength: short, memorable, obviously about apparel, obviously about running. Great as a wordmark; the "t" is a hanger hook waiting to happen.

Risk: semantically narrow — a singlet is one summer garment, and half the value here is cold-weather layering. Could read race-only to habit runners.

Recall: high  ·  Scope: narrow  ·  Warmth: high
outkitted.run

Reads as: a verb-ish state. "Kit" covers every layer, every season, plus shoes and accessories — the whole wardrobe.

Strength: scope matches the product, including future gear recommendations. "Get outkitted" is a usable command.

Risk: longer, easy to mistype (outfitted / outkit), and "out-kitted" can imply being beaten on gear. Less pronounceable on first read.

Recall: medium  ·  Scope: broad  ·  Warmth: medium

Name durability  ·  will this word survive a second sport?

A domain is a cheap, swappable asset. A name whose meaning is locked to running is not. If cycling, skiing, or trail-adjacent sports are ever on the table, the word has to travel — the TLD can simply be swapped per sport (dialed.run → dialed.bike → dialed.ski), keeping one wordmark while each vertical gets an address that looks purpose-built for it.

CANDIDATEPORTABILITYWHY
dialed TRAVELS BEST Already native slang in cycling and MTB. Sport-neutral by construction, and it names the outcome rather than the activity.
layers FULLY PORTABLE Every cold-weather sport has a layering problem. No running-specific baggage at all.
feelslike FULLY PORTABLE It's physics, not a sport. Portable, but pays the spelling tax in every vertical.
outkitted SEMI-LOCKED "Kit" travels, but skews cycling harder than running — it reads as someone else's vocabulary in both.
singlet LOCKED Running, track, wrestling — nothing else. The strongest recall on the list and the shortest ceiling.
Hold, don't build

Secure the second-sport TLDs (.bike, .ski, .cc) with the launch domain. Cheap insurance; no product commitment implied.

Don't pay today

Never soften the running-specific identity for a sport three years out. Vertical credibility now beats optionality later.

Verbal fallback

If a locked name wins on merit anyway, the expansion path is a house brand above it — not a rename of the running app.
Why dialed beat the shortlist

Perfect spelling, high recall, and zero semantic debt — the only candidate that scores well on all three.

singlet had the best recall but locked the brand to one summer garment and one sport. outkitted matched the scope but invited mistyping and read as cycling vocabulary. layers was the most durable and the most generic. feelslike was the most honest and the hardest to spell. Dialed gives up literal meaning about clothing — a tagline's job, not a name's.

02 / Logo direction

Brackets. Dialed means inside the bounds — so the identity is a notation, not a mascot.

Engineering notation for "within spec." It escapes being a logo and becomes a way of writing: every range the product shows gets bracketed, so the brand appears hundreds of times a session without ever being a badge stuck in a corner. Type-only — nothing to redraw per sport, nothing to maintain as artwork.

Bracket rules

Brackets are pink when the wordmark is the subject, and inherit the text colour everywhere else. Never both in one viewport. Tracking stays tight (−4.5%); gap between bracket and word is 0.10em.

What earns brackets

Bounded, measured values only: temperature ranges, verdict counts, confidence states, sweet spots. Never a garment name, a username, or free prose — that would make the device meaningless.

Don't

Round the brackets, outline them, gradient them, or swap them for parentheses or chevrons. No running-figure icon, no arch, no italics for "speed."

Clearspace & secondary mark

One cap-height on all sides; minimum 96px wide for the lockup. A registration crosshair is held in reserve as a wordless mark for merch and embroidery.

Still to draw

These are direction studies set in Archivo Black, not final artwork. A type designer should draw the brackets as real vectors — weight matched to the word, optical spacing, and a heavier variant that survives embroidery and screen print.

Ratio rule

60% ink or chalk, 30% one accent, 10% the other two. Never three accents fighting in one viewport.

Semantic locks

Teal always means measured conditions. Yellow always means the app is telling you something. Pink is brand and approval.

Contrast

All three accents carry ink type only — never chalk on pink or yellow at body sizes. Ink on accent clears 4.5:1.

Photography

Outfit photos are the real color in the product. Accents step back to labels and edges wherever a user photo appears.

05 / Voice & tone

Talk like a fast friend at the trailhead: certain, brief, never precious about gear.

We sound like

Declarative. Numeric when numbers help. Confident enough to make a call: "Long sleeve. Not the jacket."

We never sound like

A hype coach ("CRUSH IT"), a fashion app ("curate your looks"), or a hedging assistant ("you may want to consider").

On being wrong

Own it plainly and learn out loud. "Too warm. Noted — we'll drop the vest above 45°."

RECOMMENDATION

"41° and damp at 6am. Long sleeve, shorts, thin gloves. You'll want the gloves for two miles and then you won't."

Specific, opinionated, one dry observation. Never a list of options.

EMPTY WARDROBE

"Nothing in here yet. Add the five things you actually reach for — the rest can wait."

Lower the bar. Never ask for a full inventory up front.

POST-RUN PROMPT

"6.2 at 41°. Did it work?"

Three taps maximum. The feedback loop is the product; never make it feel like a survey.

SOCIAL / NOTIFICATION

"Dana ran your conditions yesterday. She wore less than you did."

Comparison as usefulness, with a light needle. Never shame, never leaderboards for clothing.

06 / Product principles
01

Logging must be cheaper than remembering

Associating an outfit to a run is taps, not forms. Pre-fill from the last similar run and let the user correct it.

02

Weather is never typed by a human

Conditions are derived from GPS and time, always. Automatic truth is what makes the dataset worth anything.

03

Recommend, don't present options

One outfit, with a reason, from clothes they own. Alternatives live one tap behind the answer — never beside it.

04

The feed is reference, not performance

People follow others to see what worked in weather they're about to run in. Sort and surface for usefulness before recency.

05

Gear talk stays honest

When purchase suggestions arrive, they're earned by gaps in the user's own data and clearly marked. Never dressed as a recommendation.

06

Running on the surface, sport-agnostic underneath

The engine is a wardrobe, a conditions record, and a comfort verdict — none of which are inherently about running. Keep garment categories out of running-only vocabulary and don't let the activity record assume pace-per-mile. The interface stays unapologetically for runners; the data model quietly doesn't care. Costs nothing now, saves a migration if cycling or skiing ever earns its own front door.
